Ogata Kōrin — born to a Kyoto textile merchant, the painter who gave Rinpa its name

The life of Ogata Kōrin (1658–1716) in a timeline: the Kariganeya textile house, his brother Kenzan, the rank of hokkyō and his journeys to Edo, his Wind God and Thunder God after Sōtatsu, Irises, Red and White Plum Blossoms, and the Eight Bridges screens at The Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Key points

  • Born in 1658, the second son of the Kyoto textile merchant Kariganeya. His younger brother was the potter Ogata Kenzan
  • He first studied under Yamamoto Soken of the Kanō school and modelled himself on Hon'ami Kōetsu and Tawaraya Sōtatsu
  • The name "Rinpa" comes from the "rin" of Kōrin. It is said that Sōtatsu began the style and Kōrin brought it to completion

Kōrin grew up in a large Kyoto textile house and made his living as a painter after spending the fortune he inherited. The National Treasures Irises and Red and White Plum Blossoms are both by Kōrin. A century later Sakai Hōitsu in Edo honoured Kōrin and carried on his style.

Kōrin's career as a painter began with the decline of his family's fortunes. He first studied under Yamamoto Soken of the Kanō school, later modelling himself on Kōetsu and Sōtatsu. His younger brother Kenzan is known as a potter.

Style

  • He leaves wide areas of gold and repeats flowers like stencils, giving the picture rhythm
  • He evokes scenes from classics such as the Tales of Ise through scenery alone, without figures
  • In copying Sōtatsu's Wind God and Thunder God, he changed details to make them his own

Irises appears to be inspired by the Eight Bridges episode of the Tales of Ise, in which, by a marsh crossed by eight bridges, a poem was composed placing the five syllables of kakitsubata (iris) at the start of each line. Kōrin lined up clusters of irises on gold. Flowers of almost the same shape repeat, and are thought to have been painted with stencils.

In the Eight Bridges screens at The Metropolitan Museum of Art he painted the same subject with an angular bridge running diagonally across both screens. Kōrin's Wind God and Thunder God faithfully copies Sōtatsu's National Treasure screens (Kennin-ji), but Kōrin turned the thunder god's eyes, which in Sōtatsu's work look down on the world, towards the wind god.

Irises was designated a National Treasure in 1951. On the back of Wind God and Thunder God Sakai Hōitsu later painted Flowering Plants of Summer and Autumn, with summer grasses behind the thunder god and autumn grasses behind the wind god; the two sides have been separated for conservation and are now separate screens.

Two National Treasures, one Important Cultural Property and one work in New York — Kōrin's major screens are divided between Tokyo, Atami and New York. Irises and Eight Bridges show the same scene from the Tales of Ise.

Where to see his work

The two National Treasures are divided between the Nezu Museum in Tokyo and the MOA Museum of Art in Atami. Check each museum's display schedule before going. The Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York holds Eight Bridges. The Tokyo National Museum holds both Kōrin's Wind God and Thunder God and Hōitsu's Flowering Plants of Summer and Autumn.

FAQ

Where does the name "Rinpa" come from?

From the "rin" of Kōrin. The name was long unsettled; "Rinpa" came into general use only in recent times.

How was he related to Ogata Kenzan?

Kenzan was his younger brother, known as a potter.

Which of his works are National Treasures?

Irises (Nezu Museum) and Red and White Plum Blossoms (MOA Museum of Art).

When did he die?

In Kyoto in 1716 (Shōtoku 6, 6th month, 2nd day), aged 59.

When did he go to Edo?

He first went to Edo in 1704 and travelled repeatedly between Kyoto and Edo in the Hōei era.

Who taught him?

He first studied under Yamamoto Soken of the Kanō school and later modelled himself on Hon'ami Kōetsu and Tawaraya Sōtatsu.

How does Kōrin's Wind God and Thunder God differ from Sōtatsu's?

It faithfully copies Sōtatsu's work (Kennin-ji, National Treasure), but turns the thunder god's eyes, which in Sōtatsu's work look down on the world, towards the wind god.

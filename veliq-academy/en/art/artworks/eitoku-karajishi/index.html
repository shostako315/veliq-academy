Chinese Lions — Kanō Eitoku's right screen and the left screen added later by Kanō Tsunenobu

Chinese Lions (Museum of the Imperial Collections, Sannomaru Shōzōkan, National Treasure). A pair of six-panel screens: the right screen by Kanō Eitoku, the left painted later by Kanō Tsunenobu to match it. Presented to the Imperial Household by Mōri Motonori in 1888. What it shows and what to look at.

Key points

  • A pair of six-panel screens showing a male and female Chinese lion walking among rocks on gold. A National Treasure
  • The right screen is by Kanō Eitoku (1543–1590); the left was painted later by Kanō Tsunenobu (1636–1713) to match its composition
  • Presented to the Imperial Household by Mōri Motonori in 1888 (Meiji 21)

Chinese Lions is a pair of screens whose two halves were painted by different artists in different periods. The right screen is accepted as Eitoku's work on the strength of a certificate by Kanō Tan'yū, and is one of the few works securely attributed to him. The lions fill a picture more than two metres high.

What it shows

On the right screen a male and a female Chinese lion walk calmly among rocks, drawn with powerful brushwork. The left screen was added later by Kanō Tsunenobu to match the composition of the right; the two are now handed down as a pair.

How it was made

The right screen dates from the Momoyama period (16th century), the left from the Edo period (17th century). Both are painted in colour on paper laid with gold leaf. In 1888 (Meiji 21) Mōri Motonori presented them to the Imperial Household in Yamaguchi.

What to look at in front of it

  • The curls of the mane and tail of the lions on the right screen
  • The difference in brushwork between the right and left screens, by different painters
  • The height of the screens, taller than a person

Where to see it

Museum of the Imperial Collections, Sannomaru Shōzōkan (East Gardens of the Imperial Palace, Chiyoda, Tokyo). Check the museum's website for the display schedule.

FAQ

Did the same painter paint both screens?

No. The right screen is by Kanō Eitoku, the left by Kanō Tsunenobu.

When did it enter the imperial collection?

It was presented by Mōri Motonori in 1888 (Meiji 21).

How large is it?

The right screen is 223.6 × 451.8 cm, the left 224.0 × 453.5 cm.

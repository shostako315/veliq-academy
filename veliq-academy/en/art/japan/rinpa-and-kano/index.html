Rinpa and the Kanō school — in front of the screens

Through the Kanō school, official painters for some 400 years, and Rinpa, handed down by private devotion rather than teaching: who painted Wind God and Thunder God and where it is, where the name "Rinpa" comes from, Irises at the Nezu Museum, Eitoku's wall paintings for Azuchi Castle in the Chronicle of Lord Nobunaga, and how Tan'yū moved the school to Edo.

Key points

  • The Kanō school was a body of professional painters that lasted about 400 years, from Masanobu in the mid-15th century to the 19th century. Eitoku led the wall paintings of Azuchi Castle (from 1576); Tan'yū those of Edo Castle and Nijō Castle
  • Rinpa was handed down not by teacher and pupil or by family, but by private devotion: Kōrin devoted himself to Sōtatsu, and Hōitsu to Kōrin
  • The name "Rinpa" comes from the "rin" of Kōrin, and became established with the 1972 "Rinpa" exhibition at the Tokyo National Museum
  • Wind God and Thunder God is by Tawaraya Sōtatsu and belongs to Kennin-ji. Kōrin copied it faithfully
  • The National Treasure Irises at the Nezu Museum appears to take its subject from the Eight Bridges episode of the Tales of Ise

Period and background

The Kanō school began with Kanō Masanobu, official painter to the Muromachi shogunate. Based on Song and Yuan ink painting, its style was fixed by Motonobu, who fused it with the techniques of yamato-e. Under Eitoku it painted the wall paintings of the castles of Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi; under Tan'yū it moved its base from Kyoto to Edo and became official painter to the Tokugawa shogunate. In the workshop, painters learned by copying model books (funpon), a system suited to large collaborative projects.

Rinpa is the opposite. It began with Hon'ami Kōetsu and Tawaraya Sōtatsu in the Momoyama and early Edo periods, and was carried on about a hundred years later by Ogata Kōrin (1658–1716) and his brother Kenzan, and a further hundred years later by Sakai Hōitsu (1761–1829) and his pupil Suzuki Kiitsu — people separated in time, place and social rank. Where the Kanō school learned directly from masters by copying, Rinpa is marked by intermittent succession born of admiration for predecessors.

Eitoku, grandson of Motonobu and son of Shōei, is recorded in the Chronicle of Lord Nobunaga as having painted the wall paintings of Azuchi Castle (from 1576). The castle burned and the paintings do not survive. In the 1580s he painted the wall paintings of Osaka Castle (given as 1583 or 1585) and the Jurakudai (1586 or 1587), and died in 1590. Tan'yū, Eitoku's grandson (son of Takanobu), became official painter to the Edo shogunate in 1617, and in 1621 received a residence outside the Kajibashi gate of Edo Castle and moved his base to Edo. His style was elegant, making use of empty space. The four oku-eshi houses of the Edo Kanō were Kajibashi, Kobikichō, Nakabashi and Hamachō. Sanraku's line is called Kyō-Kanō (Kyoto Kanō); Sansetsu belongs to it.

Wind God and Thunder God

According to the Kyoto National Museum, the screens bear no inscription or seal, yet Sōtatsu's authorship is not doubted. The ground laid entirely with gold leaf sets off the forms and creates a deep space. Kōrin copied Sōtatsu's screens faithfully, but turned the eyes of the thunder god — who in Sōtatsu's version looks down on the world below — towards the wind god (Tokyo National Museum, Important Cultural Property). On the back of Kōrin's screens Sakai Hōitsu later painted Flowering Plants of Summer and Autumn.

Irises

Clusters of irises on a gold-ground pair of six-panel screens. It appears to draw on the Eight Bridges episode of the Tales of Ise, and the same flower shapes are thought to have been repeated with stencils. Kōrin's Eight Bridges at The Metropolitan Museum of Art treats the same subject, with an angular bridge cutting diagonally across both screens. It was designated a National Treasure in 1951.

The name "Rinpa"

The Kyodo News glossary says the name was "taken from the 'rin' of Ogata Kōrin" and that what to call the group was long unsettled. The National Diet Library's Japan Search says the name became established through the "Rinpa" exhibition the Tokyo National Museum held in 1972 as its centenary special exhibition (catalogue Rinpa, Benridō, 1973).

How to look

Forms of succession

The Kanō school preserved its style through copying model books and dividing labour in the workshop; Rinpa artists looked at works and devoted themselves to predecessors they had never met. Set Sōtatsu's and Kōrin's Wind God and Thunder God side by side and the direction of the thunder god's eyes has changed in the copy.

Uses of gold

Wind God and Thunder God uses its gold-leaf ground to set off forms; Irises repeats the same flower shapes across gold. Where the gold of Kanō castle paintings displays authority, the gold of Rinpa is a building block of the composition.

The form of the folding screen

A pair of two-panel screens (Wind God and Thunder God) and a pair of six-panel screens (Irises) look different when folded and stood up. A screen is painted to be seen standing with its folds, not as a flat reproduction.

FAQ

How many Wind God and Thunder God screens are there?

The best known are Sōtatsu's (Kennin-ji, National Treasure) and Kōrin's faithful copy (Tokyo National Museum, Important Cultural Property).

Is Rinpa a school?

Not a school of teachers and pupils or of family. It is an intermittent lineage carried on by artists of different generations who devoted themselves to their predecessors' works.

Can Eitoku's wall paintings for Azuchi Castle be seen?

No. Azuchi Castle burned and the paintings do not survive. That Eitoku painted them is known from the Chronicle of Lord Nobunaga.

Why is there a Kanō school in both Edo and Kyoto?

Because Tan'yū became official painter to the shogunate in 1617 and in 1621 moved his base to a residence outside the Kajibashi gate in Edo (Edo Kanō), while Sanraku's line in Kyoto was called Kyō-Kanō.

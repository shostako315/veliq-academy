How to look at Buddhist statues — from Buddhas to devas

How to sort Buddhist statues into four groups — Buddhas, bodhisattvas, wisdom kings and devas — and how to read form, technique and sculptor through three examples: the Ashura at Kōfuku-ji (hollow dry lacquer, 734), the Amida at Byōdō-in (Jōchō, 1053, joined-block construction) and the Kongō Rikishi of the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji (Unkei and others, 1203, made in 69 days).

Key points

  • Buddhist statues fall into four groups: nyorai (Buddhas, who have attained enlightenment), bosatsu (bodhisattvas, who seek it), myōō (wisdom kings of Esoteric Buddhism) and ten (devas, gods of ancient India who converted to Buddhism)
  • Fudō Myōō is the central figure of the Five Great Wisdom Kings: he holds a sword in his right hand and a rope in his left, flanked by the two boy attendants Kongara and Seitaka
  • The Eight Legions at Kōfuku-ji, including the Ashura (153.4 cm high), were made for the Western Golden Hall founded in 734, in hollow dry lacquer (dakkatsu kanshitsu)
  • The seated Amida in the Phoenix Hall of Byōdō-in (277.2 cm high) was completed by Jōchō in 1053 in joined-block construction (yosegi-zukuri); it is the only surviving statue securely attributed to him
  • The Kongō Rikishi of the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji (over 8 m high) were made in 1203 by Unkei and some 20 sculptors who divided the work and finished in 69 days

Period and background

In the great temples of the Nara-period capital (8th century), statues were made in hollow dry lacquer — a clay core covered with layers of hemp cloth and lacquer — or in clay. The Eight Legions and Ten Great Disciples of Kōfuku-ji belong to the Western Golden Hall founded in 734. In the late Heian period, Pure Land belief spread together with the idea of the latter days of the Dharma; in 1052 the regent Fujiwara no Yorimichi turned the Uji villa he had inherited from his father Michinaga into a temple (Byōdō-in), and in 1053 built the Amida Hall (Phoenix Hall). At the start of the Kamakura period, during the rebuilding of Tōdai-ji after it burned in the Genpei War, the Kongō Rikishi of the Great South Gate were made in 1203. Nara dry lacquer, Heian joined-block construction and Kamakura's Unkei: the techniques and sculptors of three periods correspond to three statues.

Ashura and hollow dry lacquer

According to Kōfuku-ji, in hollow dry lacquer a wooden core is set up and the rough form is made in clay; several layers of hemp cloth are laid over it with lacquer; when dry, the back and other parts are cut open in a few places to remove the clay, boards and battens are inserted as reinforcement, and finally the surface is finished with lacquer mixed with wood powder and the like before gold leaf or colour is applied. The Ashura has three faces and six arms; bare above the waist, he wears a sash and scarf, a necklace and armbands, a skirt and plank sandals. He is one of the gods protecting the Buddha. Kōfuku-ji's Eight Legions are the eight figures Gobujō, Shakara, Kubanda, Kendatsuba, Ashura, Karura, Kinnara and Hibakara.

Seated Amida and joined-block construction

According to Byōdō-in, the sculptor Jōchō completed it in Tengi 1 (1053); it is 277.2 cm high and regarded as the perfected form of joined-block construction, a technique unique to Japan. It is the only surviving statue securely attributed to Jōchō. The 52 Bodhisattvas on Clouds, made by Jōchō's workshop in the same year, hang on the walls above the lintels inside the central hall.

Kongō Rikishi and 69 days

In Kennin 3 (1203), Unkei and some 20 sculptors divided the work and completed the two figures, each over 8 m high, in 69 days. They are the largest Kongō Rikishi among Japan's designated cultural properties, and the Great South Gate is Japan's largest Niō gate.

How to look

Sort by appearance

According to the Nara National Museum, nyorai are modelled on the Buddha and have features showing they are unlike ordinary people. Bosatsu are modelled on the Buddha before enlightenment and, because he was an Indian prince, are shown in the dress of Indian nobility. Myōō are deities of Esoteric Buddhism who take terrifying forms to guide those who do not follow the teaching, some with many faces and arms. Ten were originally gods of ancient India and are shown in the dress of Chinese nobles or generals; some are female. A simple figure wearing only a monk's robe is a nyorai; one with a jewelled crown and ornaments is a bosatsu; an angry face marks a myōō.

Identify by attributes

Fudō Myōō holds a sword in his right hand and a rope (kensaku) in his left. A sword with a dragon coiled round it is called the Kurikara sword. He is the central figure of the Five and Eight Great Wisdom Kings, regarded as a manifestation of Dainichi Nyorai, and wears flames of the karura all over his body. There is a triad form with the boy attendants Kongara Dōji and Seitaka Dōji.

Read the period from technique

Hollow dry lacquer (hemp cloth and lacquer, hollow inside) belongs to the Nara period; joined-block construction (several pieces of wood assembled) was perfected by Jōchō in the late Heian period; Unkei and others in Kamakura made colossal statues over 8 m high. The weight of a statue and its hollow interior come from these differences of technique.

FAQ

What is a statue wearing only a monk's robe?

A nyorai (Buddha). With a jewelled crown and necklace it is a bosatsu, with an angry face a myōō, and in the dress of a general or noble a ten.

How can Fudō Myōō be recognised?

He holds a sword in his right hand and a rope in his left, with flames behind him. He is the central figure of the Five Great Wisdom Kings and is often attended by the two boys Kongara and Seitaka.

What is hollow dry lacquer?

A technique in which hemp cloth soaked in lacquer is laid in layers over a clay core, and the clay is removed after it hardens. The Eight Legions and Ten Great Disciples of Kōfuku-ji (734) are examples.

Who made the Kongō Rikishi of the Great South Gate?

Unkei and some 20 sculptors, who divided the work and made them in 69 days in 1203.

How many statues are known to be by Jōchō?

Only one is certain: the seated Amida in the Phoenix Hall of Byōdō-in (1053).

## Key points

- **Raku ware** was begun in the Momoyama period by **Chōjirō**, first head of the Raku family, expressing in a single bowl the thought of Rikyū's wabi tea. His tea bowls are thought to have been made from around 1579, and the character "raku" comes from the Jurakudai palace
- The National Treasure **Shino tea bowl Unohanagaki** was fired at the Mutabora kiln in Mino and is in the Mitsui Memorial Museum. The only Japanese-made tea bowls that are National Treasures are Kōetsu's Fujisan and this one
- **Oribe ware** was made in Mino from the start of the Keichō era, and is named after Rikyū's pupil **Furuta Oribe** (1544–1615)
- Porcelain fired at Arita and elsewhere was called "Imari" after the shipping port of **Imari**; in 1977 it was designated a traditional craft as "Imari-Arita ware"
- **Yōhen tenmoku** are bowls fired at the **Jian kilns** in Fujian in the Southern Song dynasty. Only three survive — at the Seikadō Bunko Art Museum (the Inaba Tenmoku), the Fujita Museum and Ryūkō-in at Daitoku-ji — all National Treasures

## Period and background

Tea-ceremony ceramics shifted their centre of gravity from karamono, Chinese imports such as tenmoku bowls, to wamono from Japanese kilns under the wabi tea of the late 16th century. Sen no Rikyū had Chōjirō in Kyoto make tea bowls; after Rikyū's death his pupil Furuta Oribe became the leading tea master and brought new tastes to the kilns of Mino. In the early 17th century Japan's first porcelain was fired at Arita, and from 1659 the Dutch East India Company began buying it in large quantities. ==Yōhen tenmoku, regarded as the summit of karamono, and the wamono tea bowls of Momoyama were gathered to be used in the same tea rooms.==

### Raku ware

According to the official history of Raku ware, it was begun in the Momoyama period (16th century) by Chōjirō, first head of the Raku family, and Raku tea bowls are thought to have been made from around Tenshō 7 (1579). Because the Raku family lived near Hideyoshi's Jurakudai and Chōjirō's bowls came to the world through Rikyū, who had a residence at the Jurakudai, they were called "Juraku-yaki tea bowls", and came to be known as "Raku ware". Hideyoshi is said to have granted a seal with the character "raku". The Raku family continues in Kyoto today; there are records of the 15th Kichizaemon (Raku Jikinyū) and the 16th Kichizaemon.

### Unohanagaki

According to the Mitsui Memorial Museum, it was fired at the Mutabora kiln in Mino; its warped form, free spatula carving and iron painting under the glaze are said to share the taste of Oribe. It was formerly owned by the Fuyuki family of Edo and passed to Takayasu of the Muromachi Mitsui family in the mid-1890s. ==Of tea bowls made in Japan, only two are National Treasures: Hon'ami Kōetsu's white Raku bowl Fujisan and this Unohanagaki.== Shino ware is a white-glazed ware fired in Mino in the Azuchi-Momoyama period, with a thick coat of Shino glaze made from crushed and refined feldspar. Its varieties include red Shino and grey Shino; together with Seto-guro, its technique is designated an Important Intangible Cultural Property.

### Imari ware

A general name for porcelain fired at Arita and elsewhere, from the shipping port of Imari-tsu; it was called "Imari" in Japan and abroad. It is Japan's first porcelain. It was long said to have begun in 1616, but excavations suggest firing began around Keichō 10 (1605). Local documents say Ri Sanpei found the raw material for white porcelain at Izumiyama in Arita. From 1659 the Dutch East India Company bought it in large quantities. On 14 October 1977 it was designated a traditional craft as "Imari-Arita ware" (Imari, Takeo, Ureshino, Arita and Nishimatsuura district, Saga Prefecture).

### Yōhen tenmoku

Black-glazed bowls fired at the Jian kilns in Fujian in the Southern Song; black-glazed bowls from Jian are called jianzhan. Inside the black bowl, spots like stars floating in space are scattered and shine lapis blue. ==Only three survive — at the Seikadō Bunko Art Museum (the Inaba Tenmoku), the Fujita Museum and Ryūkō-in at Daitoku-ji — and all are National Treasures.==

## How to look

### Clay and glaze

The soft hand-formed surface of Raku, the thick feldspar glaze of Shino, the copper green of Oribe, the white porcelain of Imari and the black glaze of tenmoku: the five differ in material and glaze. Before picking a bowl up, look at whether it is white or black, thick or thin.

### Warped forms

The warped form of Unohanagaki and the warped Oribe bowls are intentional. Set beside the regular forms of tenmoku and porcelain, they show the taste of Momoyama.

### Provenance

A tea bowl carries a lineage of owners (denrai). Unohanagaki passed from the Fuyuki family of Edo to the Muromachi Mitsui family. The museum's record is the last line of that lineage.

## FAQ

### How did Raku ware begin?

With Chōjirō, first head of the Raku family, who in the Momoyama period expressed the thought of Rikyū's wabi tea in a single bowl. His tea bowls are thought to have been made from around 1579.

### What ware is Unohanagaki?

A Shino tea bowl fired at the Mutabora kiln in Mino. It belongs to the Mitsui Memorial Museum; the only Japanese-made tea bowls that are National Treasures are Kōetsu's Fujisan and this one.

### Where does the name Oribe ware come from?

From the taste of the tea master Furuta Oribe (c. 1544–1615), a pupil of Rikyū.

### Are Imari ware and Arita ware different?

Porcelain fired at Arita and elsewhere was called "Imari" after the shipping port of Imari-tsu. Today it is designated a traditional craft as "Imari-Arita ware".

### Where were yōhen tenmoku fired?

At the Jian kilns in Fujian in the Southern Song. All three surviving bowls are in Japan; the Inaba Tenmoku at the Seikadō Bunko Art Museum is one of them.

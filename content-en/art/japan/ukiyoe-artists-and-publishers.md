## Key points

- Ukiyo-e prints were made by a division of labour under a **publisher** who planned them: a **designer** who drew the design, a **carver** who cut the blocks, and a **printer** who printed the colours
- Full-colour **nishiki-e** grew out of a picture-calendar exchange in 1765 (Meiwa 2); publishers began selling them the following year
- **Tsutaya Jūzaburō** (1750–1797) published Utamaro's bust portraits of beauties in 1792 and, in the fifth month of 1794, issued 28 of Sharaku's actor bust portraits with dark mica backgrounds all at once
- **Sharaku** issued some 140 prints in about ten months, from the fifth month of 1794 to the first month of 1795, all published by Tsutaya
- **Hiroshige's** Hōeidō edition of the Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō has 55 sheets (Nihonbashi, the 53 stations and Kyoto), published in 1833–34

## Period and background

Until the mid-18th century, Edo woodblock prints were mostly in black ink alone or in two or three colours (benizuri-e). In 1765 (Meiwa 2) haikai poets and connoisseurs held gatherings to make and exchange picture calendars in competition, with Suzuki Harunobu at the centre. Working with carvers and printers, Harunobu developed full-colour printing and also used pigments mixed with shell white. Publishers named the result "Azuma nishiki-e" (brocade pictures of the East) and turned it into a product. ==This was the beginning of nishiki-e, which depended on a division of labour among four roles: publisher, designer, carver and printer.==

In the third month of 1791, under the Kansei Reforms, the sharebon of Santō Kyōden were prosecuted; Kyōden was sentenced to 50 days in manacles and his publisher Tsutaya Jūzaburō to the confiscation of half his property. In 1804 Utamaro was sentenced to prison and manacles for prints including The Five Wives of Taikō Viewing Flowers at Rakutō.

**Utamaro** studied under Toriyama Sekien and in 1775 drew the cover of a Tomimoto jōruri text. With Tsutaya as publisher he issued the kyōka picture books Ehon mushi erami (1788), Shiohi no tsuto and Momo chidori, and at the start of the Kansei era established the style of **bust portraits of beauties** (bijin ōkubi-e) centred on the face. As for **Sharaku's** identity, there is an Edo-period record identifying him as Saitō Jūrōbei, a Noh actor of the Awa domain, but it is not settled. **Hiroshige**, whose family name was Andō, entered the studio of Utagawa Toyohiro and was allowed the name Hiroshige in 1812.

### Sharaku's four periods

Period 1 (5th month 1794): 28 actor bust portraits with dark mica grounds. Period 2: 8 ōban sheets with mica grounds and 30 hosoban sheets. Period 3: mainly 47 full-length hosoban prints, with aiban bust portraits and sumo prints. Period 4 (1st month 1795): 10 hosoban sheets and 2 sumo prints. ==He issued some 140 prints in about ten months, then vanished.== The bust portraits of the first period are regarded as his masterpieces; Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei is one of them.

### The Hōeidō Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō

Publication began in Tenpō 4 (1833), and the Nihon Daihyakka Zensho considers that all 55 sheets were almost complete during Tenpō 5. The same encyclopaedia gives as the occasion for the series Hiroshige's journey to Kyoto in autumn of Tenpō 3 (1832) with the shogunate's procession presenting horses to the court.

## How to look

### Trace the process backwards

A nishiki-e was made in the order: the designer's drawing → the carver's key block and colour blocks → the printer's layered printing. There is a block for each colour, and registration marks are cut into the blocks so they align. Misregistration of colour at the edges of the sheet, or a glittering mica background, reveals the work of carver and printer.

### The scale of the composition

On the same ōban sheet, what was depicted changed: full-length figures (Harunobu) → face-centred bust portraits (Utamaro, Sharaku) → horizontal landscapes (Hiroshige). Bust portraits sold expression and the texture of skin; horizontal landscapes sold memories of travel.

### The publisher's judgement

Ukiyo-e, though discussed under the designer's name, were products planned and financed by publishers. Tsutaya moved from Utamaro's kyōka picture books (1788) to bust portraits of beauties (c. 1792–93) to Sharaku's actor bust portraits (1794); Hōeidō moved from joint to sole publication.

## FAQ

### Did one artist make an ukiyo-e print?

No. The publisher planned it, the designer drew the design, the carver cut the blocks and the printer printed the colours. In colour printing, a block was prepared for each colour and printed in layers.

### When did nishiki-e begin?

At the picture-calendar competitions and exchanges of 1765 (Meiwa 2), when Suzuki Harunobu developed full-colour printing with carvers and printers, and publishers turned it into a product called "Azuma nishiki-e".

### Who published Sharaku's prints?

All were published by Tsutaya Jūzaburō. His first works were 28 actor bust portraits with dark mica grounds in the fifth month of 1794.

### How long was Sharaku active?

About ten months, from the fifth month of 1794 to the first month of 1795, in which he issued some 140 prints.

### How many sheets does the Hōeidō Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō have?

Fifty-five: the 53 post stations plus Nihonbashi and the Sanjō Bridge in Kyoto.

## Key points

- Amanohashidate (Miyazu, Kyoto Prefecture) and its surroundings, seen from a height no real viewpoint could give
- It has no signature or seal, but is attributed to Sesshū from the brushwork, the composition and the handwriting of the place names
- It is made of 21 joined sheets and shows corrections, so it is thought to have been a draft

View of Amanohashidate is a single hanging scroll showing Amanohashidate, one of the Three Views of Japan, from a bird's-eye height. It belongs to the Kyoto National Museum and is designated a National Treasure. ==There is no place from which this view can be seen at once; it is thought to combine several sketches made on site.==

## What it shows

At the centre are the white sand and green pines of Amanohashidate, with Chion-ji temple at its foot. Across the Aso Sea lies the town of Fuchū, and on the mountain behind it Nariai-ji temple. At the bottom spread Miyazu Bay and the Kunda peninsula.

## How it was made

It is based on the real landscape, but the mountains are exaggerated in height and the town is stretched sideways. The height of the viewpoint matches no actual place. Cultural Heritage Online cites recent research that explains these changes as the adoption of compositions from Chinese landscape painting.

The picture is made of 21 sheets of uneven shape and shows corrections. The Kyoto National Museum treats it as a draft and notes that a finished version may have existed, though details are unknown. The brushwork is quick and rough.

## What to look at in front of it

- The line of the white sandbar and the pines along it
- The place names written on the picture, one of the clues for attributing it to Sesshū
- The joins between the sheets, 21 in all
- The mountains, rising higher than in reality

## Where to see it

Kyoto National Museum (Higashiyama Shichijō, Kyoto). It is shown only for limited periods. Check the museum's website for the display schedule.

## FAQ

### Is it signed by Sesshū?

No. It has no signature or seal, but is attributed to Sesshū from the brushwork, the composition and the way the place names are written.

### When was it painted?

Opinions differ. The Kyoto National Museum notes that, because the picture shows the pagoda of Chion-ji, the leading view dates it after the pagoda was rebuilt in 1501, when Sesshū was in his eighties. The Nihon Daihyakka Zensho also states that it has been dated to around 1501.

### Is it a finished work?

It may have been a draft, because of the 21 joined sheets and the corrections.

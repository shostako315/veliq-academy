## Key points

- A pair of landscape scrolls painted by Sesshū in ink alone on paper. A National Treasure, held by the Tokyo National Museum
- In the autumn scroll a riverside path leads into the distance towards a pavilion. In the winter scroll a sheer cliff stands in the centre
- The scenes are built so that the eye can follow objects from front to back

Autumn and Winter Landscapes is a pair of hanging scrolls in which Sesshū Tōyō painted one autumn and one winter landscape. It was handed down at Manshu-in temple in Kyoto. Each scroll is 47.7 cm high and 30.2 cm wide. ==The line of the cliff running top to bottom through the winter scroll is the first thing the eye meets.==

## What it shows

In the autumn scroll a path runs along a river into the depth of the picture, with a pavilion far in the distance. Trees and houses are gathered in the lower half, leaving the upper half as a wide autumn sky.

In the winter scroll a sheer cliff stands in the centre, with a small building below it. The trees are bare, showing the bleak season.

## How it was made

Sesshū was a Zen monk who is thought to have studied painting under Shūbun while training at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto. From 1467 to 1469 he travelled to Ming China and studied many Chinese styles. The commentary on Cultural Heritage Online notes that in this work the viewer can follow the scenery from front to back, and that the positions of objects are clearly separated — a rational construction not found in earlier landscape painting.

## What to look at in front of it

- Winter: the outline of the central cliff, a thick ink line drawn from top to bottom in one stroke
- Winter: the small building at the foot of the cliff, and the difference in scale
- Autumn: the order in which the eye moves from the trees in front, along the riverside path, to the distant pavilion
- Autumn: the width of the empty sky at the top

## Where to see it

Tokyo National Museum (Ueno, Tokyo). To protect them, paintings designated as National Treasures are shown only for limited periods. Check the museum's website for the display schedule.

## FAQ

### Who painted it?

Sesshū Tōyō, a Zen monk painter. The Nihon Daihyakka Zensho encyclopaedia states that he was born in 1420 in Bitchū (now Sōja, Okayama) and is generally said to have died in 1506.

### Where is it?

Tokyo National Museum. It is designated a National Treasure.

### What is it painted with?

Ink alone on paper (shihon bokuga), as a pair of hanging scrolls.

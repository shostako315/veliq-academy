## Key points

- In about ten months, from the fifth month of 1794 (Kansei 6) to the first month of 1795, he issued some 140 actor prints and other works
- His publisher was Tsutaya Jūzaburō. In the first month, the fifth, he issued 28 ōban bust portraits with dark mica backgrounds (kuro-kira-zuri)
- His dates are unknown. After this period he broke off contact with the ukiyo-e world

Sharaku is known for actor prints showing kabuki actors large from the chest up. The Nihon Daihyakka Zensho counts 142 sheets and notes theories of 143 to 145. ==The contemporary Ukiyo-e ruikō, by Ōta Nanpo, records that because Sharaku tried too hard to paint the truth he did not last, and was finished within a year or two.==

## Life and periods of work

| Period | Content |
|---|---|
| Period 1 (5th month 1794) | 28 ōban bust portraits with dark mica backgrounds |
| Period 2 (7th–8th months 1794) | 38 sheets, mainly full-length actor prints |
| Period 3 (11th – intercalary 11th month 1794) | 64 sheets, including 47 full-length hosoban prints and 11 aiban bust portraits |
| Period 4 (1st month 1795) | 12 sheets: 10 full-length hosoban prints and 2 aiban sumo prints |

As to his identity, Saitō Gesshin's Zōho Ukiyo-e ruikō describes him as a Noh actor of the Awa domain, but the Nihon Daihyakka Zensho says this has not been confirmed. There is also a record that he lived at Jizōbashi in Hatchōbori and died before 1818 (Bunsei 1).

## Style

In Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei at the Tokyo National Museum, the jutting chin and the eyes glaring upward are said to be designed to pair with a print of the opposing role. The hands have long been pointed out as awkwardly drawn, but this is said to convey murderous intent.

## Where to see his work

The Tokyo National Museum holds Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei. Ukiyo-e prints are sensitive to light and are shown only for short periods; check the museum's website.
